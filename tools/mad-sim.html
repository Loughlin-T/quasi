How it works

The target is a point dipole. Its field is B = (μ0/4π)[3(m·r̂)r̂ − m]/r³, and a total-field sensor records its projection onto the Earth's field direction, ΔT ≈ B·F̂. That projection is why the same target gives a different-looking signature at every latitude and heading.

For a straight pass, every possible signature is a combination of three Anderson functions uk/(1 + u²)5/2, k = 0, 1, 2, with u the along-track distance divided by the range at closest approach. The detector slides a window along the record, projects the data onto those three functions (after removing a local linear trend), and sums the squared coefficients. For white noise that statistic is χ² with three degrees of freedom, which sets the threshold for the chosen false-alarm probability. The window is matched to the true CPA range here; a real system runs a bank of windows.

Noise is synthesised as white sensor noise, a 1/f component built from a bank of first-order filters, and slowly varying platform residual. Because coloured noise is not white, the realised false-alarm rate will differ from the nominal one. Re-run the noise a few times to get a feel for it.
